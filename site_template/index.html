Machine learning project · Pima diabetes data, 768 women

{{project_name}}

Can six routine measurements tell which women are likely to develop diabetes in the next five years, well enough to decide who should get a follow-up blood test? This started as a college project that claimed 81% accuracy. Rebuilt and tested fairly, that turned out to be luck. This is the honest version: a model you can try, what it gets right and wrong, and how it was built.

How it works

Five steps, from raw data to a decision about one woman.

  1. Collect768 women, eight health measurements each, and whether each was diagnosed with diabetes within five years.
  2. CleanZeros that really mean "not measured" are treated as gaps. Insulin and skin thickness, missing too often, are left out.
  3. LearnThe model learns how each measurement relates to diabetes. Glucose, weight and age may curve rather than follow a straight line.
  4. Test fairlyIt is scored 50 times, always on women it hasn't seen, so the results aren't flattered by luck.
  5. DecideFor a new woman it gives a risk. At or above the cut-off ({{cut_pct}}), she is flagged for a follow-up test. The cut-off comes from a stated cost: here, a missed case is treated as {{pub_ratio}} times as bad as a false alarm.

What would change this?

This explains the model, not her health. The model describes patterns in 768 women; it does not say that lowering a reading would lower anyone's risk. Only glucose and BMI are tried, one at a time, within a modest limit ({{cf_glu_lim}} mg/dL lower, or BMI {{cf_bmi_lim}}% lower).

Where should the cut-off go?

Every cut-off is a trade. Lower it and more women with diabetes are caught, but more healthy women get tests they don't need. Move the slider, or pick a preset: 2×, 5× or 10× means a missed case is that many times as bad as a false alarm. The picture shows what happens to every 100 women screened. This is not a medical tool. The data is 768 women of Pima heritage aged 21 and over, collected near Phoenix, Arizona and published in 1988. The model has not been tested on anyone else. It is here to show how a risk model is built and judged, not to assess anyone's health. It has since been run, unchanged, on one outside group of women. It ranked them well but its risk numbers were far too high (Story 12). The page also tells you when the numbers you enter are unlike the women the model learned from (Story 13).

What we found

The main results, each with the chart behind it. For every conclusion, how solid it is and where the evidence is, see the findings. Unfamiliar word? Try the glossary.

1. The original 81% was luck

The college project tested its model once, on one random fifth of the women, and got a good draw. Running exactly the same method 200 times, each time on a different random fifth, gives anything from {{lucky_min}} to {{lucky_max}}. The fair answer is the average, {{lucky_mean}}. For scale: answering "no diabetes" for everyone is 65% accurate and catches nobody.

2. Accuracy hid the real problem

For screening, what matters is how many women with diabetes are caught, and how many healthy women are sent for tests they don't need. With the usual cut-off of 50%, the model misses {{p100_fn_def}} of every {{p100_pos}} women with diabetes. Lowering the cut-off to {{cut_pct}} misses only {{p100_fn}}, but raises false alarms from {{p100_fp_def}} to {{p100_fp}} in every 100 women. There is no free lunch: the right cut-off depends on what a missed case costs compared with an extra blood test. Story 8 shows how to turn that into a number.

3. Half the insulin readings were never taken

The data uses zero for "not measured", and nobody has an insulin level of zero. {{insulin_n}} of 768 women have no insulin reading and {{skin_n}} have no skin-thickness reading. Six ways of filling the gaps all score the same; when real readings are hidden and guessed back, the best method recovers only {{rec_insulin}} of the truth; and for {{flips}} women the screening decision depends on the guess. Even where insulin was measured, it added nothing. So the model leaves both out.

4. Simple beats complicated here

Ten models went through a fair contest. Each could tune its own settings, but only on training women, so the test women never influenced the result. The simple, explainable model the page uses (curves for glucose, weight and age) scored {{m4_pub}}. None of the more complex methods beat it: a neural network, XGBoost, a random forest and a blend of four models all scored between {{m4_low}} and {{m4_ens}}. With 768 women and six measurements, the limit is what the data can tell us, not the choice of method. The model also beats a simple rule: flagging everyone with a glucose of 140 or more finds {{rule_tp}} women with diabetes, and the model, raising about the same number of false alarms, finds {{model_tp_rule}}.

5. Risk peaks around 50, then falls

In this data the diabetes rate climbs from 14% in women aged 21 to 24 to 71% at 50 to 54, then falls to 28% at 60 and over, partly because many women who would develop diabetes already have it by then. The first models drew a straight line through age and kept predicting about 50% for the oldest women. Letting age curve fixed that: with each model's cut-off set to catch 85% of cases, false alarms fell from {{fp_m2}} to {{fp_screen}} across all 768 women, and the share of healthy women 45 and over correctly not flagged rose from {{spec_old_m2}} to {{spec_old}}.

6. Blood sugar matters most, by far

Going from a low glucose reading to a high one (86 to 167) multiplies the odds of diabetes about 25 times. Weight and age come next, at about 5 to 6 times each. Family history and pregnancies matter a little, and blood pressure barely at all once the others are known. These are patterns in this group of women, not proof that one thing causes another.

7. The risks can be trusted, but not equally for everyone

When the model says 30%, about 30 in 100 such women did develop diabetes, so its numbers mean what they say. It is less sharp for women 45 and over, partly because half of them have diabetes. Under the old rule (catch 85% of all cases) it caught only {{sens_young}} of cases among women under 30, down from {{sens_young_m2}} with the earlier model, because it gives young women lower, more accurate risks. Choosing the cut-off from costs fixes most of that: at the page's {{r5_cut}} cut-off, {{r5_young}} of the cases under 30 are caught. Separate cut-offs for each age group did no better (cost per 100 women, in false-alarm units: {{band_each}} against {{band_single}} for one shared cut-off), because the risks are about equally accurate in every age group, so the same cut-off is right for all of them.

8. Where the cut-off goes is a question of cost

Statistics can say what each cut-off does. Only a person can say how bad a missed case is compared with an unnecessary blood test. If a missed case is {{pub_ratio}} times as bad as a false alarm, the cheapest cut-off is 1 divided by 6, about {{r5_cut}}, and that is what the page uses. The old rule (catch 85% of cases) quietly assumed a missed case is only about {{old_ratio}} times as bad. Per 100 women screened, a cost-based cut-off does this:

The curves are flat near their lowest point, so the cut-off does not need to be exact, but they are not flat everywhere: the wrong cost ratio means a visibly worse cut-off.

9. Rebalancing the data doesn't help

Only 35% of the women have diabetes, and a common recipe for that kind of lopsided data is to rebalance it: give the rarer group more weight, or invent extra cases for the model to learn from (SMOTE). It did not help here. The model's ranking of women was the same, and catching 85% of cases still took about {{fa_pub}} false alarms ({{fa_cw}} with class weights, {{fa_smote}} with SMOTE). What changed is that the risks became untrustworthy: the average predicted risk rose from {{risk_pub}} to {{risk_cw}} and {{risk_smote}}, when the real rate is 35%. Moving the cut-off does everything rebalancing does, without distorting the risks.

10. Why the model gives the number it does

Glucose, BMI and age are the only inputs the model clearly leans on. Scrambling a woman's glucose reading costs the model {{perm_glucose}} of AUC, BMI {{perm_bmi}}, age {{perm_age}}; scrambling pregnancies, blood pressure or family history costs about 0.01 or less, and for each of them the range includes zero. A boosted-tree model, which has no tidy formula to read, ranks the six inputs in the same order (rank agreement {{boost_rho}}), so the ranking is about the data and not the type of model. SHAP, a popular explanation method, reproduces this model's exact contributions to within {{shap_diff}} on the log-odds scale, which is the check that the method is working.

The shapes matter as much as the sizes. Risk climbs steadily with glucose. It rises with age to about 50 and then falls. BMI is the odd one: it rises sharply up to about 30 (the odds multiply by {{bmi_up_24_30}} from BMI 24 to 30), then flattens. Whether that flat stretch is real was a question left over from the model contest. A resampling test says the levelling-off is genuine, because a steady rise would have multiplied the odds by about {{bmi_steady}} from BMI 30 to 42 and the data rule that out; but the data can't say whether the stretch is exactly flat (the odds from BMI 30 to 42 are between ×{{bmi_low_30_42}} and ×{{bmi_high_30_42}}). Replacing the BMI curve by a straight line scores {{bmi_dauc}} AUC lower, which is within chance (p = {{bmi_p}}).

The box under the risk estimate above turns this into a question about one woman: what is the smallest change in glucose or BMI that would take her below the cut-off? At the page's cut-off, {{cf_flag_per100}} in 100 women are flagged, and the typical flagged woman is well above it (median risk {{cf_risk_median}}). So a realistic change clears only {{cf5_either}} of the flagged women (glucose alone clears {{cf5_glucose}}, BMI alone {{cf5_bmi}}); among those with a risk under 30%, {{cf_below30}} can be cleared. That is the honest shape of the answer: most flagged women are not a small change away from the cut-off.

11. How sure is the model about one woman?

A risk of 40% looks exact, but it is an estimate made from 768 women. To see how much it could move, the model was refitted 200 times on resamples of those women, and each woman's risk read from every refit. The middle 95% of those readings is her range. It is {{iv_median}} points wide for the typical woman: narrow for low risks ({{iv_low}} points under 10%) and widest in the middle of the scale ({{iv_mid}} points for risks of 30% to 60%). At the page's cut-off, {{iv_cut_inside}} of women have the cut-off inside their range, so whether they are flagged could go either way depending on which 768 women had been studied.

Is that range honest? Two checks. In a simulation, where the true risks are known, the range contained the true risk {{cov_boot}} of the time on average ({{cov_boot_low}} to {{cov_boot_high}} across simulated studies): a little short of the 95% on its label, so read it as "about 90%". And its size agrees with how much estimates actually vary between different training sets of the real data (ratio {{spread_ratio}}, correlation {{spread_corr}}).

A range around a risk says how much to trust the estimate. It can't say whether a woman will develop diabetes, because she either will or won't. For that there is a second tool, conformal prediction. It turns the risk into a call of "no", "yes" or "too uncertain", with a guarantee: if you ask for the 90% level, at least about 90% of women without diabetes are called "no" or "uncertain" (never wrongly "yes"), and at least about 90% of women with diabetes are called "yes" or "uncertain". Checked on women the rule had never seen (300 random splits), it held: {{cf90_cov0}} and {{cf90_cov1}} at the 90% level, {{cf80_cov0}} and {{cf80_cov1}} at the 80% level.

The price of the guarantee is the uncertain group: {{cf90_unc}} of women at the 90% level, {{cf80_unc}} at 80%. At the 90% level the rule says "no" for {{cf90_no}} of women and about {{cf90_nowrong}} in 100 of them have diabetes anyway; it says "yes" for {{cf90_yes}} and about {{cf90_yeswrong}} in 100 of them are healthy. The guarantee is about each real group, not about each call, which is why the "yes" calls are less reliable than the "no" calls: the women with the highest risks still include many without diabetes.

12. Does it work on women who are not in this data?

So far every test used the same 768 women. To find out how the model behaves elsewhere, the finished model, with nothing changed, was run on {{x_n}} women from NHANES, a US national health survey, who had the same two-hour glucose test. They are not much like the Pima women: older (median age {{x_age_nh}} against {{x_age_pima}}), lighter, and with fewer pregnancies, so much so that a simple model can tell the two groups apart with AUC {{x_domain}}. And only {{x_pos}} of them ({{x_prev}}) have diabetes by the definition used here, against {{x_prev_pima}} of the Pima women. That definition is a blood test result (HbA1c of 6.5% or higher) or a doctor's diagnosis, which is not the same as "diagnosed within five years", so this is a test of behaviour away from home, not a like-for-like repeat.

The ranking held up. The model puts women who have diabetes above women who don't with AUC {{x_auc}} (95% range {{x_auc_lo}} to {{x_auc_hi}}), in line with 0.85 on unseen Pima women. But the glucose reading alone scores {{x_auc_glu}}, so on this data the model adds nothing measurable beyond glucose.

The risk numbers did not. On average the model predicts {{x_pred}} and {{x_prev}} actually have diabetes. Women it puts in the top band, at {{x_top_pred}} on average, are {{x_top_obs}} in fact. Most of the gap is that the model learned from a group where 35 in 100 have diabetes. A single rescaling learned on part of the NHANES women fixes the average (the model's error score falls from {{x_brier}} to {{x_brier_recal}}, against {{x_brier_avg}} for quoting everyone the average), but that needs outside women with known outcomes.

The cut-off shows the same thing. At the 5 times cut-off ({{x_flag5_pima}} of Pima women are flagged), the model still catches {{x_sens5}} of the cases in NHANES, but it flags {{x_flag5}} of all women, about {{x_fa5}} in 100 get an unnecessary test, and only {{x_prec5}} of the flagged women have diabetes. A cut-off chosen from costs in a group where 35% have diabetes does not suit a group where 4% do.

By age and ethnic group the ranking looks fine wherever it can be measured, but each group has only 3 to 17 women with diabetes, so that is weak evidence. The risks are too high in every group. What carries over is the ranking. What doesn't is the level of the risks and the cut-off. Differences in how the data was collected can't be separated from differences between the women.

13. Is there anything hidden in the data?

Every test so far used the diabetes label. This one left it out while looking for two things: natural groups of women, and odd records. The women do not fall into groups. A flat map of them (the two directions of greatest spread keep only {{y_pc2}} of the variation) shows one cloud, and splitting them into groups scores {{y_sil2}} for the best split against {{y_null2}} for data with no groups in it at all. The groups also do not hold steady when the women are resampled (best stability {{y_stab_max}}, where 0.8 would be reliable). Where groups do appear they restate what we know: older, heavier, higher-glucose women are more likely to have diabetes. Adding group membership or an oddness score to the model does not help (AUC {{y_auc_member}} and {{y_auc_odd}} against {{y_auc_base}}).

The odd-record search finds the readings you would expect. With all eight columns, the skinfold of 99 is number {{y_skin_rank}} on the list of most unusual women (of 768), the insulin of 846 is number {{y_ins_rank}} and the BMI of 67 is number {{y_bmi_rank}}. Removing the {{y_removed}} most unusual women from training changes the model's AUC by {{y_trim_diff}}, which is nothing, so they stay in and are recorded.

The one use that did come out of it is the notice under the risk meter. Women far from the typical Pima woman get a wider range of possible risks ({{y_w_odd}} points against {{y_w_rest}}), and {{y_nh_out}} of the NHANES women from Story 12 would trigger the notice. It tells you the estimate rests on little data. It does not correct the risk: the NHANES women who triggered it were predicted at about the same risk as the rest, but {{y_obs_flag}} of them had diabetes against {{y_obs_typ}} of the others.
